Zuckerberg wrote 6,500 words today.

The short version: spread AI out to everyone instead of locking it in a few labs.

He's not just saying it. Meta open sourced a new model today called Muse Glimmer. Their flagship model opens up in a few weeks. A billion dollars going to the towns hosting their data centers.

Then the part that got me. He told the White House their 30 day review window for new models is too slow. A lab asking the government to move faster, not slower.

I've spent two years watching labs ask for more guardrails in public while racing each other in private. This is the opposite play.

Maybe it's genuine. Maybe it's positioning. Probably both, same as everything else in this industry.

Either way, a 30B model just became something you can run on your own laptop for free.

Which version protects the small business owner adopting AI more: the tool they can own, or the one they rent forever?
LI . NO. 02
Harvard and MIT built 8.3 billion AI agents this week.

Not to write code. To mirror actual human beings. Background, psychology, behavior, lifestyle, all the way down to a 1,290 dimension profile per agent.

Then they ran them through surveys and chatbots and websites and checked how close the agents matched real people.

91.5% consistency.

That number is the whole story. Market research used to mean a $5,000 invoice and three weeks of waiting on real respondents. If a synthetic panel gets you there at 91.5%, that invoice doesn't make sense anymore for a lot of businesses.

Would someone pay for that? I think someone would pay for that. Probably a lot of someones.

I'm not saying real customer conversations stop mattering. They don't. But the expensive middle step, the part where you pay to guess what people think before you ask them, that part's getting cheaper fast.

What's the first research question you'd hand to a synthetic panel instead of a survey company?
LI . NO. 03
Alibaba let AI agents run an online store for a full simulated year.

No humans. Just agents making the calls. Inventory, pricing, marketing, the whole thing.

Final result: 27.3% of what a human operator would've made.

That's not a failure. That's useful data. The agents were fine at the task in front of them. They struggled with the thing that isn't a task, the day after day adjusting, the noticing when the plan stopped working and needs a new plan.

Every week someone pitches a fully autonomous AI-run business like it's already here. It's not. Not this year.

What's actually here is an agent that's great at one job, running on a loop, with a human deciding when the loop needs to change.

That's still a massive unlock. It's just not the headline version.

If you're building with agents right now, where's the line between what you'd trust the agent to decide and what still needs your eyes on it?
