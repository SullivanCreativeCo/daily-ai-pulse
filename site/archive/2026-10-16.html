LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
Here's a pricing idea I wish I'd heard sooner.

Don't charge for hours. Charge for the savings.

Danny Laflamme sells a small business an AI setup. If it saves them $10,000 a month, that's $120,000 a year. He takes 10% of that. $12,000.

But he spreads it over four months, not twelve. Half down. A weekly 45-minute call. After four months they own the system and he's done.

Why that works: a monthly subscription means you owe them something every month, forever. A four-month project is concentrated work, and people churn way less when the end is in sight.

And by month four, he's found a dozen other things they need. That's the next proposal.

The number isn't magic. The reason is. You can point at the savings and say "this is why."

Would a small business pay for that? I think a small business would pay for that.

What's the one task your clients complain about most?
LI . NO. 02
Stop saying you sell AI employees.

Nobody wants one. They want the thing they hate doing to stop landing on their desk.

Corey Ganim laid this out well. Take a roofer who gets 40 leads a week. Most take a day or more to answer. If an agent drafts the reply in 5 minutes, that roofer makes money immediately.

That's the pitch. Not the tool. The 5 minutes.

Here's what people skip, though. The hard part isn't the agent. It's loading the context. The SOPs, the examples of what good looks like, the brand voice. Without that it sounds like AI and it breaks.

So the real work is writing down how the job gets done. Boring. Also the reason clients keep paying every month.

That's not just a service, that's a weight removal product.

What's a task in your business that happens five times a week and nobody likes doing?
LI . NO. 03
A new model drops and the benchmarks look great.

Then people try it on real work and the story changes.

Meta's Muse Wonder is a good example. On paper it's right up near the top. Then Riley Brown used it for five hours and went back to Codex and Claude Code. Meta's own Alexander Wang said it plainly: try it on your actual coding work, not the 3D game demos.

I like that. It's the honest version.

The question has shifted. It's not "which model is best." It's "where does this one fit in what I already do."

So here's what I'd do. Take one real task you do every week. Run it through whatever's new. Compare the output to what you already get.

Ten minutes. Way more useful than a leaderboard.

What's the one task you'd use as your test?
