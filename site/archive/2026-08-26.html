LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
Two companies shipped the same idea this week and didn't know it.

Wix launched Symphony, a team of AI agents that runs your business and gives you a morning rundown like a manager checking in.

Sabi launched an AI chief of staff you manage over text messages. No app. No login. No prompt engineering course.

Neither one is a chatbot. Both are trying to be a hire.

That's the shift. For two years the pitch was here's a powerful tool, go figure out what to ask it. Now it's here's someone on your team, go delegate.

Small business owners never wanted a tool. They wanted the work off their plate. That's it. That's the whole model.

Would someone pay for an employee that texts them the important stuff and handles the rest? I think a lot of people already are.

If you're building anything for small business owners right now, the question isn't what can my AI do. It's would a real employee handle it this way. If the answer's no, rebuild it.
LI . NO. 02
Rocket Money's new AI agent doesn't have an app.

Rowan watches your spending, texts you when it finds a way to save, and you just reply like you're texting a friend. It renegotiates your bills. Cancels subscriptions. Sets up savings transfers. All from a thread you already have open.

Everybody's been racing to build the better dashboard. Better charts, better AI chat window bolted onto the side.

Rowan skipped all of it and went straight to the interface people already trust: text messages.

That's not a small decision. A dashboard asks you to open an app and think about your money. A text meets you where you already are, mid-scroll, between everything else.

The best AI interface for most people isn't a new interface. It's the one they never had to learn.

What's the one part of your business you'd actually want to run over text instead of another tab?
LI . NO. 03
IBM gave away a reasoning model this week. Free. Apache license. Runs coding agents out of the box.

That's Granite 4.2. Three sizes, 3B up to 30B parameters, and the two bigger ones spent training time inside actual sandboxed environments. Writing code. Running a terminal. Searching the web. Getting corrected when they got it wrong.

Not simulated. Actually doing the thing, then graded on whether it worked.

Here's why that matters if you're not IBM's target customer. The gap between expensive frontier model and good enough open model keeps shrinking, and it's shrinking on a license that lets you build a product on top of it without asking permission.

Two years ago the moat was access to a good model. Now the moat is what you build around one.

Married to an idea is a great way to get forgotten. Same logic applies to whatever model you built your product on.
