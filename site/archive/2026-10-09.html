Today's headline

Google's new Gemini agent can be set up as a coworker with its own email address, calendar, Drive storage and a spot in the company directory.

LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
Google's new work agent gets its own email address.

Not your login. Its own. Calendar, Drive storage, a spot in the company directory. Its edits show up under its name in version history.

That's the part I keep thinking about.

When an agent sends mail as you, nobody can tell later what you did and what the software did. When it has its own account and its own permissions, you can treat it like a new hire. Give it a role. Limit what it can touch. Read the log when something looks off.

It's in private preview, so most of us can't try it yet. But the prep is boring and doable. Write down the jobs that repeat every week. Quote follow-ups. The Monday report. Sorting the shared inbox.

Then ask of each one: what would I let a new hire touch on day one?

Would I hand over the whole Drive? Because that's what a lot of small companies are about to find out they did.
LI . NO. 02
Two numbers from this week that don't fit together.

The top 1% of AI buyers spend $903 a month. The median paying customer spends $25. And only 2.2% of US households pay for AI at all.

So 98% haven't bought. Either they're early or they're never going to.

I think it's neither. I think nobody has shown them the boring thing.

Nobody wakes up wanting an agent. But picture a cleaning company owner who exports the schedule every Friday to work out payroll in a spreadsheet. That person wants Friday afternoon back.

That's not an AI product. That's a weight removal product.

Look at what the 1% actually pay for. Tools that build, automate and create. Things that remove work.

The other 98% might pay for exactly the same thing. They just won't call it AI.

Would someone pay to make that Friday disappear? I think someone would pay for that.
LI . NO. 03
A new frontier model lands roughly every 11 days now.

That's the number from the AI Daily Brief this week, and it matches what my feed looks like.

Published benchmarks won't help you much here. A lot of them are in the training data by now. And even the clean ones can't tell you how a model feels on your work.

So build a small one. Five or six tasks you actually do. A LinkedIn post. A pricing pushback reply. A weekly plan. Write down what a good answer looks like before you run anything.

Then run every new model through the same tasks, blind, and score them yourself.

Nufar Gaspar did this live and was surprised by which models won her LinkedIn task. She'd guessed wrong, and that's the point. Your gut about the logo on the model is not data.

What are the five tasks you'd put in yours?

New harnesses & tools

  • docker-agent - LavX News Define an agent in YAML: model, instructions, tools. Run it with docker agent run. Teams of sub-agents can delegate to each other, and you push and pull agents through any OCI registry. It ships with Docker Desktop 4.63+. Builders care because agents become versionable files you can hand to a client the way you'd hand over a config. https://news.lavx.hu/article/docker-launches-docker-agent-an-open-source-ai-agent-framework

  • LOOP - CIOL Soket AI's open-source harness is built for agents that work for a long time. You can branch, pause and resume sessions like version control, and it keeps history through context compaction. It's a developer preview on GitHub, written in Rust, with sandboxing still on the roadmap. Worth a look if your agent runs die halfway through a big job. https://www.ciol.com/news/indiaai-backed-soket-ai-launches-loop-for-long-running-ai-agents-12659054
