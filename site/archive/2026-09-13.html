LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
Four different companies just shipped the same product.

OpenAI opened their Agents API to every developer this week. It's the plumbing behind Codex, now something anyone with an API key can call.

Here's the thing nobody's saying out loud. Anthropic did this in April. Google did it in April too. Microsoft's version lives inside Azure. Four labs, one year, basically identical infrastructure.

That tells you something. When everyone converges on the same layer at the same time, the layer stops being the advantage.

The advantage moves up a level. It's not who has agents anymore. It's what you actually build with one, and whether it solves a problem someone's willing to pay to have solved.

I've watched this pattern before. The tool gets commoditized fast. The idea built on top of it is what survives.

So if you're waiting for 'the right agent platform' before you start, ask yourself something. Which platform even matters anymore?
LI . NO. 02
DeepSeek just retired their own flagship model. On purpose.

V4 Pro was their big model. Bigger parameter count, more compute, the flagship.

Then V4.1 Flash shipped. Smaller, cheaper, and it beat V4 Pro on their own benchmarks. So DeepSeek did something most companies won't do. They routed all the V4 Pro traffic to the cheaper model and started charging Flash pricing for it.

Nobody made them do that. They looked at their own product line and admitted the expensive one wasn't earning its price anymore.

That's rare. Most of us keep the bigger, more expensive version around because we built it, because it took months, because killing it feels like admitting something.

Do more with the same. Not the same with less.

Worth asking about your own stack, your own service tiers, your own offer. Is there something you're keeping alive because you built it, not because it's still the best answer?
LI . NO. 03
Meta's new AI agent can spend your money.

Muse shipped this week. It's a personal agent that can browse the web, manage apps, and finish financial transactions on your behalf. Book the flight. Buy the thing. Handle it.

Meta knows the trust problem here is real, so they built it inside something called a Secure VM, sandboxed per user, with the browsing logic kept separate from the decision logic. Next up is a 'Confidential VM' tier built with Moxie Marlinspike, the guy who built Signal, where even Meta can't see what the agent does.

Here's what I keep coming back to. The technology to let an agent act for you is basically here now. The bottleneck was never capability. It's trust.

That's true for a company shipping to a billion phones. It's just as true for a small business owner deciding whether to let AI touch their calendar, their inbox, their client replies.

Would you let it? At what point does the convenience outweigh the discomfort of not watching?
