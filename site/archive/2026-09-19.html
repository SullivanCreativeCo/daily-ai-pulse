Today's headline

No fresh launches hit the wire today. But three different podcasts landed on the same idea from different angles, and it's worth catching.

AI is splitting into two tracks right now. One track is getting simpler: Claude merged its products this week, Meta shipped Muse, everybody's racing to remove the step where you have to pick a mode before you start.

The other track is getting weirder, and way more useful if you build things. It's an AI that never writes a single word. It just decides. Fast, cheap, built to run quietly behind whatever you're already doing.

That second one is the one worth understanding today.

LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
Most of what people call AI doesn't write a single word.

I watched a demo this week of a tool called Jev. You feed it an email, and instead of asking it to "read this and tell me what to do," you give it a set of choices. Spam or not. Urgent or not. Worth a reply or not.

It doesn't generate text. It just picks, with a confidence score attached.

1,700 real emails, sorted by category, priority, and spam score, in a few minutes. Total cost: 18 cents.

That's the part that stuck with me. Every chatbot wrapper I've seen this year is trying to have a conversation. Most business problems aren't conversations. They're sorting problems. Is this lead hot or cold. Is this review a real complaint or noise. Does this invoice need a human or not.

You don't need a chatty model for that. You need something fast, cheap, and boring that just decides.

What in your business is actually a sorting problem wearing an AI costume?
LI . NO. 02
Nobody pays for a GitHub repo. They pay for what stops happening once it's running.

Saw a breakdown this week of an open source DocuSign alternative. Free to run. Costs about a fifth of what DocuSign charges at scale. The guy selling it isn't pitching "open source contract software." He's pitching "your leases get signed in minutes instead of days, and your DocuSign bill disappears."

Same software. Completely different sale.

This is the part people skip when they get excited about AI tools. The tool was never the hard part. Explaining why someone's life gets easier because of it, that's the hard part. That's the actual product.

I think about this every time I look at a piece of free infrastructure and assume nobody would pay for it. Wrong question. The question is: what pain does this quietly remove, and would someone pay to never think about that pain again?

I think someone would pay for that.
LI . NO. 03
Claude just deleted a decision nobody wanted to make.

Up until this week, you had to pick: am I chatting with Claude, or am I co-working with Claude. Two different modes, two different mental models, before you'd even typed anything.

Now it's just one box.

Meta did the same thing with Muse. Someone showed their 70-year-old dad the product and he had it draft a contract, update it, and text it to a customer, no tutorial needed.

Neither company made the model smarter. They fixed the fork in the road you hit before you could use it.

That's the part I keep coming back to when I build anything for small business owners. It's never the intelligence that's missing. It's the extra step where they have to guess which button starts the thing they actually want.

Simple isn't the feature. Simple is what happens once you stop making people choose.
