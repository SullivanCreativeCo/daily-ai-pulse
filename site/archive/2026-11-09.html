LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
Check your repo before you read anything else today.

Claude Code saves the shell commands you approve into a local settings file. Per one writeup going around, that file doesn't get added to your project gitignore on its own. Credentials have ended up on public GitHub that way.

Think about why. You approve a command once, it has a key inline, and Claude remembers it so it stops asking. Super convenient. It's also a quiet copy of your secret sitting in a file you never opened.

I'm not saying panic. I'm saying open the folder.

Look in your .claude directory for the local settings file. Read what's in it. Make sure it's ignored. If a key is in there, rotate it, because deleting the file doesn't unpublish a commit.

What gets me is that the legal side of AI safety gets all the headlines. The "did I just leak my own key" side gets almost none.

Boring habit that fixes most of it: keys live in a .env, never in a command line.

When's the last time you actually read your own settings files?
LI . NO. 02
Three unrelated products shipped this week and they all made the same call.

A digital workforce tool for construction and law firms. A contract platform. A little proposal pipeline a small agency in Sydney open sourced.

Same design in all three. The agent does the work. A person signs off before anything goes out.

WorkTelli hands you the evidence first, then makes the deliverable once you approve the evidence. Docgility won't turn a suggestion into a redline until a human has looked at it. The Sydney one lets code compute the price and run the checks, and the model only gets to say what it noticed.

That last rule is my favorite. The model decides what to look at. The code decides what happens.

If you're building AI for small business owners, this is the pattern. Owners don't want a robot holding the keys. They want a fast assistant who brings the stuff and waits for the nod.

Would someone pay for that? I think someone would pay for that.

Where in your workflow does the nod actually belong?
LI . NO. 03
Five Chinese labs shipped models in about a week. Nobody on my feed is talking about the price tags.

Ant's Ling-3.2-flash runs $0.30 in and $0.90 out per million tokens, reads images and video, and claims to beat Claude Opus 5 on three vision tests. LG put out a model with a free API. Tencent's Hy4 does computer use. Baidu dropped a 280B base model. Moonshot's Kimi K3.1 Pro is open weights.

Big caveat. Almost every score is self-reported. Ant says itself there's no independent index yet. Treat the benchmarks like a restaurant reviewing itself.

But here's what I care about. For a small business tool, the question isn't which model is smartest. It's which one is good enough at a price where the client's bill doesn't flinch.

Read the Kimi license before using it commercially. Ling has no open weights yet.

Test one on a real task from a real client. Not a leaderboard. Their actual invoices, their actual inbox.

Do more with the same, not the same with less.
