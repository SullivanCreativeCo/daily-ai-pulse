LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
The way you do your job is worth writing down.

Two things happened this week that point the same direction. Anthropic is letting tool connections hand Claude the instructions along with the tools. And Google DeepMind gave a lightweight model a good set of chemistry skills, and it kept up with the big models on their test.

Same idea both times. Good instructions carry a lot of weight.

Think about the thing you keep re-explaining. How you quote a job. How you answer the "do you have availability" email. How you follow up after somebody goes quiet.

Right now that lives in your head. So every time you want help, from a person or a model, you start from zero.

Write it down once. Plain words. The steps, the exceptions, the stuff you'd tell a new hire on day one.

If it's repeatable, it's a skill.

And you don't need to be technical to write one. You just gotta know your own work, which you already do.

LI . NO. 02
98% sounds like done. It wasn't even close.

Anthropic just shared how they rebuilt their main API in a new programming language, with Claude doing a lot of the building. The first version of the core piece took about a week.

In April it was passing about 98% of its tests. Then they checked it against real traffic. Only about a quarter of inputs matched.

Why? Real people do weird stuff. One example was as small as how an emoji got written down. No big dramatic bug. Just a few bytes that were different.

They got there in the end. But the work was in the checking.

This matters even if you never touch code. Say you set up an AI to answer customer questions. You try five clean questions and it nails all five.

Then a real customer sends three questions in one sentence, a typo, and a photo of their receipt.

Building got fast. Knowing it works is still on you.

What would your messiest customer message do to it?
LI . NO. 03
Somebody at the table is gonna ask if AI is taking their job today.

And if you're the "AI person" in the family, all eyes swing to you somewhere between the turkey and the pie.

The AI Daily Brief did a whole episode on this, and one idea stuck with me. Invite people in, even if they don't like it one bit.

I like that a lot. Because the temptation is to win. Pull up a stat, talk fast, make Uncle whoever feel silly for being nervous.

But being nervous makes sense. Some jobs are gonna change. Some of that is gonna hurt. Pretending otherwise just tells people you aren't listening.

So maybe ask a question first. "What part of your week do you wish you didn't have to do?"

Now you're talking about their life instead of a headline. You might even show them something on your phone that helps with it.

We're all just figuring this out as we go. That includes the person holding the gravy.
