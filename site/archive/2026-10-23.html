LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
Your website might have a new customer who can't see it.

Greg Isenberg put it best this week. An agent is a customer with a credit card and no eyes.

Think about what that means.

The hero video you paid for? Invisible. The animated banner? Invisible. The countdown timer on the sale? Same.

What it does read is your prices, your shipping costs, your return policy, your product details. That's the whole shopping trip.

And right now Amazon is blocking Meta's Muse agent. Delta says it works with zero outside agents. Yelp wants agents to pay to get in. Some stores let them through by accident, some on purpose.

So a small business has an odd little job coming. Make the boring facts easy to read.

Price on the page. Shipping stated plainly. Return policy someone can actually find.

It's the same work that helps a human skim. It just matters more now.

Would a customer with no eyes know what you sell and what it costs?
LI . NO. 02
Anthropic's new agent can't book you a flight.

That's on purpose.

Claude Duet launched yesterday, and Anthropic was clear about what it's not. It's not for errands. It's not for tickets. It won't show you its browser as it works.

What it does: builds a daily briefing, makes dashboards, suggests actions from your data, and runs tasks in the background on its own virtual machine. Then it comes back when it needs your okay.

Muse is going broad. It hit 10 million US downloads in 40 days, and a lot of that was ad spend.

Duet is going narrow. Professionals and small businesses.

I think narrow wins for the owner of a five-person shop. Nobody running a landscaping crew needs help finding concert tickets. They need Monday's numbers waiting for them before the first truck rolls.

The boring agent is the one that stays open.

What's the one task you'd happily hand off tomorrow, and why haven't you?
LI . NO. 03
Somebody's API key just became money.

OpenCode got hit this weekend. About 1.4% of console users had their stored keys used to burn through tokens. Bills ran from hundreds to thousands of dollars. The founder's own accounts got attacked too.

Here's the part that stuck with me.

The attackers didn't steal data to sell. They used the keys directly. They stole a service and got paid in tokens.

That changes what a key is. It's not a password anymore. It's closer to a debit card with no PIN.

So anything that stores other people's keys is acting like a bank now. Whether it meant to or not.

If you build automations for small businesses, you probably touch keys all the time. Their email. Their CRM. Their AI accounts.

Where do those keys sit? Who can see them? Could you rotate every one tonight if you had to?

Not a fun thing to find out at 2am.
