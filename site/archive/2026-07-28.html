LI . NO. 01
A Chinese lab just gave away a model that probably cost hundreds of millions of dollars to train.

Moonshot released the full weights for Kimi K3 this week. 2.8 trillion parameters. Free download. Benchmarks close to Claude and GPT-5.6, and it wins some of them outright.

Here's why that matters more than the specs. For years the pitch was: pay the big labs for intelligence, or spend a fortune building your own. That gap is closing fast. When a frontier-class model is free to download, modify, and build on, the smart move stops being "which API do I pay for" and starts being "what do I build on top of the free thing."

Most people building AI products aren't training models. They're wrapping them. That job just got a lot cheaper.

What are you building on top of a model instead of trying to build the model itself?
LI . NO. 02
Induction Labs taught a model to use a computer without ever telling it what to do.

No labeled actions. No "click here, then here." It just watched 18 years of screen recordings and figured out the pattern. Photon-1 used 30 times less compute than Google's comparable model and still came out ahead.

Here's the part worth sitting with. Most small businesses have never written down how they actually work. They've just done it, over and over, in their heads.

That's not a gap. That's training data nobody's collecting yet.

What are you already doing for yourself that someone else would find useful? Record the screen for 90 days instead of writing a manual nobody reads. Now you've got something a model can actually learn from.

The SOP was never the point. The pattern was.
LI . NO. 03
Pentesting used to take a month. Now it takes a day.

Cobalt just launched an autonomous pentest that returns findings in 24 hours instead of the usual monthly or quarterly cycle. Same week, PortSwigger built agentic AI directly into Burp Suite, with a rule: the agent does the work, the human still holds the boundary. Same week, Microsoft rolled out red team, blue team, and green team agents running continuous security checks.

That's three separate companies landing on the exact same shape in one week. Speed goes way up. Judgment stays with a person.

I live in this corner of AI every day, and the thing that stands out isn't the speed. It's that nobody's trying to remove the human from the loop. They're trying to remove the human from the parts that were never judgment calls to begin with.

Where's the line for you, between the part AI should own and the part a person should still sign off on?
