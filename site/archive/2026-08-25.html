Today's headline

The AI assessment ladder is the cheat code everyone's ignoring.

Corey Ganim just laid out the math: $20 Claude subscription, 50 text messages, 98% margins, 10K/month. Free mini assessment (filter) → $999 paid assessment (positioning) → $1-2K retainer (recurring). Anyone can do this. Most people won't, because they'd rather build a platform.

LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
Everyone shipped agent orchestration this week.

LexisNexis rebuilt their legal assistant. Smartcat dropped an AI Chief of Staff. UiPath launched Maestro Flow. All the same shape: a coordination layer that picks the right AI specialist for the task, sequences their work, and reports back.

And I kept thinking: this is just... how humans organize work.

You don't need one genius. You need one thing directing five competent people. That's team structure. We've built this pattern for decades. Now AI needs it.

The pattern everyone's launching today, we already knew worked.

The question isn't "should we orchestrate?" It's "why did it take until 2026 to make it explicit?"
LI . NO. 02
Corey Ganim just made $10K/month selling AI sound boring.

Free mini assessment (foot in door). $999 paid assessment (position yourself). $1-2K retainer (recurring money). Text 50 people you already know. Four paying clients later, you're there.

Cost? Twenty bucks a month for Claude.

Margins? Ninety-eight percent.

Tools built? Zero.

Meanwhile, everyone's shipping agent orchestration platforms, multi-step workflows, and governance layers. All real. All valuable. All solving different problems.

But Corey's solving the actual problem: business owners don't know where to start with AI. They're drowning in work. They need help now, not a platform to eventually help them.

Simple wins when it solves the right problem.

What problem are you solving, and how simple is your answer?
LI . NO. 03
Thomson Reuters just proved the frontier model arms race is over.

They spent $40 million over two years building an LLM trained on Westlaw, Practical Law, and Reuters data. Not training from scratch on the internet. Training on content they own, for problems they understand.

The frontier model race costs billions. TR's defense? We own the content. We own the tools. We own the expertise no one else has.

And they're right.

The next era isn't about who has more parameters or more compute. It's about who has the defensible data and domain knowledge to make a model actually work in their space.

That's not a scale game. That's a moat game.

The question isn't "can I build an LLM?" It's "do I own something no one else does?"

If the answer is yes, you've got something real.
