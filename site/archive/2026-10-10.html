Today's headline

Small business AI agents showed up all at once this week. Meta's Muse for Small Business is free, Town is going after the inbox, and Traddie's Jodie handles trade admin.

The part to sit with is that none of them just act. They draft, show you, and wait for the yes. The agent isn't the hard part anymore. Deciding what it's allowed to touch is.

LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
Meta just made an AI agent for small businesses, and it's free.

It plugs into Shopify, QuickBooks, Canva, Stripe, Slack. You ask what you're missing on your Instagram and it tells you. It can look at sales, build ad campaigns, review cash flow.

So the agent is no longer the hard part.

The hard part is permissions.

An analyst at Coresight said it plainly. Using one of these without thinking through what you're giving it permission to do could cause a disaster.

Who sees the books? Can it spend ad money on its own? Can it email a customer before you read it?

Most owners won't ask until something goes sideways. They'll click connect, connect, connect.

That's the gap. Not building the agent. Sitting next to an owner and deciding what it's allowed to touch.

Would someone pay for that? I think someone would pay for that.
LI . NO. 02
Asana's browser agent got 76x cheaper and 5x faster in their tests.

No magic model. The agent was resending its entire history at full price on every request. The instructions were cached. The growing pile of page text and screenshots wasn't.

The workflow fix alone was about 29x. Switching models got the rest.

Here's the part I like more than the savings. With a bigger memory budget, one setup went from finishing 3 of 18 runs to all 18. Same model. It was just forgetting what it was doing.

When an agent is slow, expensive or flaky, the model is rarely the first suspect. Look at what you're feeding it, and how many times you're paying to feed it the same thing.

Before you swap models, read what each call actually sent.

What's the part of your stack you've never actually looked at?
LI . NO. 03
A guy on Corey Ganim's podcast says his company tracked a million dollars in its first month brokering company data to AI labs.

Average deal around $300K. One reportedly hit $10 million. Those are his numbers, and he's selling this, so take them that way.

But here's what stuck with me. The labs don't want a pile of files. They want to watch how work moves. A client email comes in, the team talks it through in Slack, they pick a fix, it goes back to the client.

His own business mostly ran on phone calls. Pick up the phone, call the homeowner. None of it was recorded. To an AI it just looked like ticket created, ticket closed.

A lot of small businesses run exactly like that. The real process lives in people's heads and on their phones.

Which means the stuff worth money, to a buyer or to your own AI, is usually the stuff nobody wrote down.

What are you already doing every day that nobody's captured?
