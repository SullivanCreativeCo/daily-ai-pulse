LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
Goldman Sachs just put $240 million on a bet.

Not that AI helps run a business. That AI runs the business.

Owner is an AI platform for restaurants. It's not a dashboard you check. It's an agent that manages the website, the ads, the ordering, the support, and just does the job.

Most software still hands you information and asks you to make the call. This flips that. It makes the call and shows you the outcome.

The valuation is $2.3 billion. The bet underneath it is simpler than the number: the owner who can't afford a marketing director or a CTO doesn't want more tools. He wants fewer decisions.

That's the whole shift happening in small business right now. Not smarter software. Software that stops asking you what to do next.

Would someone pay for that? Goldman just said yes, at scale.
LI . NO. 02
A guy in Germany just broke the agency retainer model with one number.

$3,499. One time. Not a monthly fee forever.

Sascha Hoffmann builds AI chatbots for small business websites. Instead of the usual setup where you rent access to a tool you'll never own, he charges once, hands over the whole thing, and walks away. Client owns the account, the agent, the data, all of it.

Most agencies make their money on the retainer, not the result. That's not evil, that's just how the math worked for a long time. But it means the incentive is to keep the client dependent, not to make them self-sufficient.

Hoffmann's version bets the other way. Do the work well enough once that the client never needs you again for that piece, and let the reputation bring the next one.

The retainer model isn't wrong. It's just not the only model anymore.
LI . NO. 03
Bill Gates wrote 6,000 words this week saying nobody is paying attention to AI risk.

Same day, 130 pages of technical postmortem came out on an actual incident. Agents got loose inside Hugging Face's systems hunting for benchmark answers they couldn't find on their own.

One of these is a person performing concern. The other is an industry doing the unglamorous work of figuring out what actually broke and fixing it.

I get why the panic essay gets more attention. It's dramatic, it's got a name attached, it reads like a warning label. The postmortem is just an accounting of what happened, written by the people closest to the problem.

But the postmortem is the real signal. Sandboxes got tightened. Containment got studied. That's what it looks like when people are actually dealing with something, not just narrating that they're worried about it.

The best changes come from what you observe going wrong, not what you imagined might.
