LI . NO. 01
Three different podcasts dropped this week showing the same business model working across three completely different industries.

Rachel: Free sandwich boards in restaurants. Sells ad placement. Upsells into web dev and AI services. $225K/year business, six months in.

Corey: Two calls per month plus Voxer plus a Notion hub. Hit $8K MRR in ten days from six conversations. Clients don't want courses. Don't want black-box agencies. Want a partner sitting beside them.

Billy: Replaced his intern with Grok Bot agents. Runs a 6,000-person newsletter. Costs $200-300 a month.

Same starting point. Same tool access. Same playbook across all three.

Fix what's broken in the business. Use an agent tool to solve it. Charge the owner to be the translator between the tool and their actual work.

The tool is table stakes now. The money is in the translation.
LI . NO. 02
Slack Code just made AI agents multiplayer and that changes how teams ship code.

Until now, most agent work was invisible. You ask Claude Code or Devin to build something, it works in the dark, hands you something finished.

Slack Code puts the agent in a channel where your whole team watches the diffs in real time, steers the work as it happens, sees the plan laid out in dedicated tabs. The work is open. The audit trail is searchable.

That sounds like a feature. It's actually a speed lever.

The moment your team can see what the agent is doing and nudge it mid-stream, the feedback loop compresses. You're not waiting for a finished thing you have to debug. You're watching it build and catching issues before they compound.

Faster shipping. Better code. Teams learning what the agent is doing instead of just trusting the output.

That's worth shipping for.
LI . NO. 03
Hour9 just published something that stuck with me.

They took eight clients looking for AI automation. After mapping workflows, only one actually needed AI.

The other seven needed traditional automation or structured code. Simpler. More predictable. Easier to maintain.

Kevin Smida said it plainly: "AI has to earn its place. If it's not the right tool, we won't recommend it."

That's the take that matters right now.

Most business owners are tired of "AI solves everything." They don't need better AI. They need clarity on what's actually broken.

Once you map the workflow, the fix becomes obvious. And half the time that fix isn't an agent. It's a process change. Or a script. Or just writing the decision rules down clearly for the first time.

The real business opportunity isn't in tools getting smarter. It's in knowing when to use them and when not to.

That's the skill that compounds.
