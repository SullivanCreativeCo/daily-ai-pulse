LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
OpenAI just shipped a model that can find security vulnerabilities nobody's found yet.

Not theoretically. GPT-6 Astra scored 100% on ExploitBench and crossed into what OpenAI calls "Critical" cybersecurity capability. Their own framework. Their own admission.

Here's the part that actually matters for anyone running a small business online.

For years the pitch on AI was "it helps you build faster." True. Also true now: it helps whoever's poking at your systems move faster too.

That's not a reason to panic. It's a reason to stop treating security like a line item you'll get to eventually.

I've watched plenty of businesses ship a website, a form, a payment flow, and never once ask who's checking it. That gap used to be slow to find. It's not slow anymore.

The tools that build your business and the tools that could break it just got the same upgrade.

Who's actually checking your stuff before someone else does?
LI . NO. 02
Salesforce just built a door out of its own house.

Claudeforce lets sales teams update pipeline, prep for meetings, and run CRM tasks straight from Claude. No dashboard. No logging into Salesforce for a lot of what reps do daily.

That's a strange move for a company that built its entire value on being the interface everyone lives inside.

But it tells you where this is going. The product isn't the screen anymore. The product is the data, the permissions, and the rules sitting underneath, with whichever AI you're already using as the front door.

Every piece of software your business pays for is about to have this same identity crisis. The UI they spent a decade perfecting stops being the moat. The API and the permission structure become the whole business.

If you're picking software right now, that's the question worth asking. Not "how's the dashboard." How well does it play with an AI agent asking on your behalf.

Would you rather own the interface, or own what's underneath it?
LI . NO. 03
Three iPhone photos. That's the input.

Li Fei-Fei's team just showed Atlas, a model that takes a handful of images and predicts what the scene looks like from any angle you didn't shoot. Not stitching. Predicting.

Old 3D reconstruction needed hundreds of photos and a lot of patience. This needs three and a few minutes.

I came up shooting weddings and product photos, so this one actually stopped me. The bottleneck in visual content has always been coverage. You can't be everywhere, can't shoot every angle, can't afford the crew for a full 3D capture.

A model that fills in the angles you didn't get changes the math on what one photographer, one small crew, one phone can deliver.

It's early. Preliminary dynamic capabilities, by their own words. But the direction is clear: less capture, more prediction, and the gap between a full production shoot and a phone in your hand keeps closing.

Would someone pay for a full 3D walkthrough shot on one iPhone? I think someone would pay for that.
