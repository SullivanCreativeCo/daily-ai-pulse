LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
Agoda, Bol, and Zendesk all shipped the same idea this week.

Three companies, three completely different businesses, same play. An AI assistant that lives inside the app you already have open. Not a new tab. Not a new login. It just does the thing.

Books the trip. Finds the product. Closes the ticket.

That's the tell that a technology moved from novelty to normal. Nobody announces "we added a chatbot" anymore. They quietly put an agent inside the product and let it work.

Small businesses are behind on this mostly because what gets marketed to them is a $500 replatform. The actual opportunity is smaller than that. Find the one task your customers already do inside your site or app, and let AI finish it for them right there.

Would someone pay to skip five steps instead of doing all five themselves? I think someone would.
LI . NO. 02
There's a new 35 billion parameter AI model out and almost nobody outside AI Twitter noticed.

It's called Occamy. Built by a small lab, trained on top of an existing open model, tuned specifically for agent work. Research, coding, file handling, the long multi-step stuff that takes a hundred moves instead of one.

Here's the part that matters if you build anything. The weights are free. The training code is free. The whole stack used to train it is free.

That's not charity. That's a bet that the next wave of agent products gets built by people who don't want to pay per token to a closed API forever.

Doesn't mean you rip out what's working today.

Means you've got an out if the pricing on your current stack ever turns on you.
LI . NO. 03
Zendesk says its new AI agents automate up to 80% of workflows. I want to see whose workflows.

Big automation numbers get thrown around constantly right now, and most of them describe the easiest 20% of the job, not the whole thing.

The more useful number came from a smaller story buried the same week. A hormone therapy practice called SottoPelle put their provider communication into one system and it saved them about 100 hours a month, same lean team, no new hires.

Notice the difference. One is a headline percentage. The other is a specific team, a specific bottleneck, a specific number of hours back.

Next time you're deciding if an AI tool is worth it, skip the vendor's big claim and ask the boring question instead. What exact task ate an hour last week, and does the tool do that task, start to finish, without you checking its work behind it?

That's the whole evaluation. Everything else is marketing.
