Today's headline

GoDaddy launched Airo AI Builder. You describe a site or app, it builds it, and plans start at $9.99 a month.

LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
GoDaddy just launched an AI builder that makes a full website from a conversation.

Plans start at $9.99 a month.

So here's the thing about that. Building the site is no longer the hard part. It's barely a part at all.

Think about a plumber or a salon owner. They get a site in an afternoon. Great. Then a lead fills out the form at 9pm and nobody answers until Thursday.

The site was never the problem. The gap between "someone showed up" and "someone got back to them" was the problem.

GoDaddy's own data shows service businesses are a big share of what's getting built. Health, home, business services. People with no tech team and no time.

They're about to have a lot of sites and the same old gaps.

Would someone pay to have the gap handled? I think someone would pay for that.

What's the first thing that falls through the cracks in your business once the website exists?
LI . NO. 02
Everybody wants AI agents to be teammates.

Give them a name. Give them a role. Org chart them.

The AI Daily Brief made a good case today that this is the wrong mental model. The companies actually doing this well, Stripe, Shopify, Ramp, Uber, don't treat agents like coworkers.

They treat them more like consultants you bring in for a job.

And the thing they all obsess over is context. Ramp pulls Slack, Notion, Linear and Calendar into memory every day so an agent never starts cold. Odyssey wrote a clean doc for every decision, so the agent knows what was decided and why.

That's the unlock. Not a better name for the agent. A better briefing.

Same as a human consultant. If you hand them nothing, you get a generic answer. If you hand them the whole story, you get something useful.

So what would your agent need to know to actually do your job for a day?
LI . NO. 03
Zapier showed an example today that I think every small business owner should see.

A new Google review comes in. An AI drafts a reply. It emails the draft to you.

Nothing posts until you post it.

That's it. That's the whole pattern.

Most owners I talk to aren't scared of AI doing the work. They're scared of AI saying something dumb to a customer under their name.

Draft, don't send.

The one- and two-star reviews even text you right away, because those are the ones that need a real person.

The agent handles the blank page. You handle the judgment.

CVS is doing the same thing at a huge scale. Agents handle the clicking through systems. People handle the calls that need an actual brain.

I think that's the right split for most small businesses right now.

What's one task you'd hand off if you knew you could still approve it first?
