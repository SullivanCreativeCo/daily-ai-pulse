LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
A 1.6 trillion parameter model just shipped and it never touched an Nvidia chip.

Meituan, the Chinese food delivery company, open-sourced LongCat-2.5 this week. MIT license. Built for agents that work through long coding tasks. The sample deployment guide runs on 194 Huawei Ascend chips and doesn't mention Nvidia once.

Meanwhile Washington is getting ready to tighten chip export controls.

Here's the part I keep chewing on. The whole bet behind export controls is that limiting the chips limits the capability. But if a lab can train something close to the frontier on its own hardware, the controls might be teaching them to build a different path instead.

I'm not saying that settles the policy argument. I don't know enough to call it.

But the model cost about 27 cents per million input tokens at launch, it plugs into Claude Code and Codex, and anybody can download the weights.

So if you build on top of these tools, the question changes. It isn't "which lab wins." It's "what do I do when the cheap option is this good?"

What would you switch if a model like this matched what you're paying for today?
LI . NO. 02
OpenAI shipped ChatGPT Wallet this week. Your agent can buy things for you.

Here's the part I actually care about. You approve every single purchase. Face ID or a fingerprint. The agent never sees your real card number.

That's the feature. Not the buying. The asking.

Constant Contact did the same thing with its new marketing tool. It builds and runs the campaign, and the owner keeps final approval.

Two different companies landed on the same design in the same week. That isn't an accident.

I've been helping small business owners think about this for a while. The ones who get nervous about agents aren't scared of the tech. They're scared of waking up to something they didn't sign off on.

So the agents that win trust are the ones that make the approval step easy, not the ones that skip it.

Fast to answer. Slow to spend. Quick to hand off.

That's what a good new hire does too.

Where in your business would you want an agent that asks first?
LI . NO. 03
Most people pick the wrong first job for an AI agent.

They go for the big, impressive thing. The thing that sounds good in a meeting.

A better way to pick it: write down four or five tasks your team does every week. Then ask four questions.

Does it come up a lot? Does it follow clear rules? Is the answer easy to check in a few seconds? Is a mistake cheap to fix?

A wrong delivery estimate is an apology. A wrong refund is money. That's why refunds stay with a person.

There's a number from NiCE's briefing this week that backs this up. They say AI can now run about 16 hours on its own at a 50% success rate.

Read that again. Half the time, after 16 hours, a human still has to step in.

So the agent isn't the whole job. The setup around it is.

Start with the boring, frequent, checkable one. Prove it works. Then add the next.

What's the one task on your list that gets all four yeses?
