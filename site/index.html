Today's headline

The companies that make your tools can now teach Claude how to use them.

Less babysitting prompts. But now you're letting somebody else's instructions into your agent, so read what you plug in.

LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
The best AI instructions are probably already in your head.

This week Anthropic made it so software companies can ship playbooks right alongside their tools. So Claude doesn't just get access. It gets the "here's how we do this" part too.

That's the part most of us skip.

We hand AI a task with zero context, get something bland back, and figure the tool isn't that smart. But you wouldn't hand a new hire your inbox and walk off.

Think about how you quote a job. What you ask first. What you never promise. When you follow up, and how you say it without sounding desperate.

You do that every week. You've just never written it down.

Start with one. Talk it out loud on your drive, clean it up, and give it to whatever AI you use.

If it's repeatable, it's a skill.

What's the thing you keep re-explaining?
LI . NO. 02
Your next idea might be sitting in somebody else's bad reviews.

Chris Koerner is taking a swing at QuickBooks with a product called Lazybooks. Before building, he had AI tools read through hundreds of reviews and tell him what people hated.

Two things kept coming up. Bank connections breaking, and a bill that kept climbing.

Then he asked real business owners which features they couldn't live without. Reports came out on top. People wanted to know if they made money.

663 signups in the first eight days. Revenue so far is zero, because the first 60 days are free. He said that part out loud, which I appreciate. And he had an audience to launch to, so keep that in mind.

The research move still works at any size. Pick the tool your industry complains about. Read the complaints. Find the one that shows up over and over.

Would someone pay for a simpler version of that? I think someone would pay for that.
LI . NO. 03
Give AI the paperwork before you give it your first impression.

I was reading about Makena Finger Zannini, who runs a company called The Boutique COO. Her team built AI tools for the stuff around selling. Outreach, proposals, follow-ups, scheduling.

She says the admin time dropped to nearly zero, so the sales team spends that time on actual people.

Then they tried AI for decks and flyers. Underwhelming. People could tell, and it didn't sound like them.

I love that she said both.

Because the reason to use this stuff isn't to crank out more stuff. It's to get your hours back for the part only you can do. The coffee. The phone call. Remembering their kid had a game on Friday.

Do more with the same, not the same with less.

If you got five hours back this week, who would you spend them on?
