Gusto just let you run payroll by talking to Claude.

Add a bonus. Update hours. Pull labor costs into a budget sheet.

Here's the part I care about. Claude prepares all of it, and the owner has to approve before anything actually runs.

Draft by the AI. Decision by the human.

A guy at Ox Asphalt is pulling timesheets into Claude to see which crew ran long, then using that to fix his scheduling. Real data instead of gut feeling.

Same week, another tool launched as an "AI coworker" for quotes, emails and posts. The best take I read on it was boring on purpose. Nothing sends, posts, pays or quotes without a human approval trail.

That's the move for anybody selling AI to small businesses too.

Start with drafts. Keep a log of what it prepared, what it used, who approved it, and what changed.

And if reviewing the draft takes longer than doing the task yourself, the tool isn't ready yet.

So before you connect AI to anything that touches customers or money, what's the one thing it's never allowed to do on its own?
LI . NO. 02
Anthropic opened a beta where one agent writes the plan and hands pieces to up to 1,000 sub-agents.

Their test: 116,000 lines of code with 70 bugs planted in it. The multi-agent setup found 66 across three runs. A single agent found somewhere between 14 and 27 per run.

Same model family. Different setup.

That's the part that sticks with me. The gap wasn't a smarter brain. It was a better division of labor.

Which, if you've ever run a small team, isn't news. One person doing everything misses stuff. Five people with clear lanes catch it.

Now the honest caveat. They didn't share cost or time. It bills on tokens plus eight cents an hour of session time, and hundreds of agents burning tokens could get expensive fast. So I don't know yet if it's practical for everyday work.

But I'd watch it. The interesting shift is the AI designing the workflow instead of me drawing the flowchart.

Would someone pay for a checker that never gets tired? I think someone would pay for that.
LI . NO. 03
OpenAI's revenue was about 50 billion annualized. Not 68.

The 68 number floated around last month. Turns out it came from an investor, not OpenAI. The investor was trying to compare apples to apples with Anthropic.

Here's why the numbers don't match. Anthropic quotes gross revenue, including what gets passed to partners like Amazon and Microsoft. OpenAI reports net, after the split.

Neither one is lying. They're counting different things.

And Wall Street reacted like OpenAI missed an earnings report. Nvidia down 3%. Oracle down 5.5%. On a number the company never published.

I keep coming back to the boring lesson. When somebody hands you a number, ask what's inside it.

Gross or net. A day or a month. Booked or collected.

Same goes for "traffic is up 40%." Up from what, measured how?

Anthropic's audited numbers are coming, and the same question lands on them.

What's the number in your world nobody's ever asked the definition of?
