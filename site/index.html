Today's headline

Meta launched Muse, a personal AI agent. You text it what you need, it makes a plan, and it does the work on its own secure cloud computer and browser. It's free for most everyday tasks and rolling out in the U.S. now.

Meta says Muse never sees your passwords or card numbers, and purchases use a one-time card number. It also says chats stay out of its ad systems.

The same day, a podcast pitched mining Facebook groups with Muse to build matching agencies. I'd want to know what Meta's rules say about that before building a business on it. It's a fun idea, but it's still a guess.

LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
Somebody went looking for a night nurse for their newborn.

The agency they called admitted how it works. They read Facebook groups, find nurses in Miami or New York, and charge a fee to connect you.

Now Meta launched Muse, a personal agent that makes a plan and does the work on its own cloud computer and browser. A lot of people are going to hear that story and see a business.

Maybe they're right. A matching agency with nobody reading groups all day is a real margin story.

But the agency was never really selling the reading. It was selling the part where a scared new parent trusts the person who shows up at 2am.

The agent can find her. It can't be the reason you let her in your house.

So if you build this, build the trust part first. Who vets? Who picks up the phone when it goes sideways?

Which part of your business is the reading, and which part is the trust?
LI . NO. 02
A small business owner doesn't want AI.

They want the thing that used to eat three hours of somebody's week to just be done.

Pax8 launched 8os and 8bot this week. 8os is how the IT partners who already manage a client's tech can deploy and govern AI workers across all their clients. 8bot is a packaged AI coworker they can resell.

Here's the part I'd read twice. Usage and cost get tracked per user and per customer. Hard caps stop runaway bills. The partner sets the price.

That's the whole reason it works for small shops. Nobody wants a surprise bill from a robot.

It's early access now, with general availability planned for early next year.

I keep coming back to this. The winners with small business won't be the ones with the smartest agent. They'll be the ones who make it boring, capped and handled.

That's not just a service, that's a weight removal product.

What's the one task a local shop owner you know would pay to never touch again?
LI . NO. 03
Your clients' customer lists are the cheapest marketing channel they have, and most never touch them.

Corey Ganim walked through eight open source repos this week. The one that stuck with me is Listmonk, a free open source email tool that works like Mailchimp.

The offer isn't "I set up email software." Nobody cares about that.

The offer is: let's get your old customers back through the door. A three email comeback sequence, two campaigns a month, to a list you already paid to build.

His numbers are $750 to set up and $400 a month. Those are his estimates, not something I've tested. Treat it like napkin math.

But look at the shape. Dentists, gyms, med spas with thousands of past customers sitting in a CRM doing nothing.

The owner doesn't care about GitHub. They care that Tuesday's schedule has holes in it.

Would someone pay for this? I think someone would pay for this.

What's sitting in your clients' systems that nobody has touched in a year?

A model cheats less in testing. Good news, right?

In some recent tests, researchers saw that drop and wondered if the model got better, or just got better at hiding it because it knew someone was watching.

That's why this week's interpretability essay matters. Nick Cammarata says most of the inside of these models is readable after all. Not everyone is sold. Google DeepMind's Neel Nanda called it interesting but not a big update.

I'm glad both sides showed up. Still don't know who's right.
TH . NO. 02
A guy rents an empty church in a strip mall, cleans it up, and rents it out for birthdays and baby showers. About $16K a month, part time, no employees.

Three churches rent it on Sundays and cover his rent. A QR code on the wall turns every party into more parties.

He spent three years looking at venues before he signed one. The places he toured were still booking on paper calendars with highlighters.

Sometimes the whole opportunity is somebody else's clunky process.
TH . NO. 03

But the direction keeps going one way. Smarter, cheaper, closer to your laptop.

If your whole offer is "I can use AI," that gets cheaper to copy every month. Knowing the customer's actual problem doesn't.
