LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
Four companies shipped the same feature this week.

ChatGPT, Meta's Muse, Cursor, and Gemini's Canvas all let you describe an app in plain words and get something working.

So the building part is getting cheap. Fast.

The TechRadar writer who tried six of these in ChatGPT said the good results came when she guided it carefully, not when she treated it like magic. She also pointed out that a slick interface isn't the same as a trustworthy answer.

That's the part that stays human.

A tool can draw you a scheduling dashboard. It can't know that your HVAC client's real problem is that the office manager is retyping the same job notes three times a day.

That knowledge comes from sitting across from someone and asking what their week actually looks like.

I think the next stretch belongs to the people who know the business, not the people who know the tool.

What do you know about your customers' week that no app builder can guess?
LI . NO. 02
A guy named James quit his nonprofit job with zero clients and zero revenue.

His first client came from chasing down a landscaping truck on his bike.

He thought he was in trouble. James just wanted to talk. He asked what the business needed, and offered to register the LLC, redo the truck branding, and build a website for $300.

That's it. That was the start.

Sixteen months later he has around 60 clients and told Koerner he made $18,000 last month.

What gets me is how small the first yes was. Not a big launch. Not a clever offer. One guy, a real problem, and a price that felt fair.

That's not just a service, that's a weight removal product. Somebody had a suspended Google profile and no website, and James took that off his plate.

What are you already doing for yourself that someone else would find useful?
LI . NO. 03
Here's a definition of AI slop I can't shake.

It's work where the person who should have done the thinking skipped it, and the cost moved to whoever receives it.

Not "bad writing." Not "sounds like a robot." It's about who ended up doing the work.

One study the AI Daily Brief guest cited says about 40% of US desk workers got something like that in the past month, and it cost close to two hours on the receiving end.

I like this definition because it gives you a test you can run on your own stuff.

Before you send it, ask whether you'd actually read this if it came to you.

If the answer is no, you didn't save time. You borrowed it from the person on the other side.

The tool isn't the problem. Skipping the thinking is.

What's one thing you sent this week that you'd skim if someone sent it to you?

THREADS / IG CAPTIONS

TH . NO. 01
A grocery store owner in Ohio gets a catalog from his wholesaler every week. He used to highlight the items he wanted, then spend a couple hours typing them into an ad.

Now he takes a photo of the pages and Muse writes it. About 15 minutes.

Not a revolution. Just two hours of his Tuesday back.

I think that's what this looks like for most small businesses. Quiet, boring, and a little bit life changing.
TH . NO. 02
Menlo's survey says enterprise AI spend is almost $177 billion this year.

It also says 35% of enterprises still aren't using AI at all.

Both things are true at the same time. Everyone online acts like it's already everywhere, and a third of the business world hasn't touched it.

That gap is where a lot of the real work is. Somebody has to sit with them and show them one small thing that works.
TH . NO. 03
Anthropic wrote about agent memory this week. The idea is that agents reread their own logs when they're idle and clean up their notes for next time.

Basically sleep. Your brain replays the day and keeps what matters.

I don't think that's only an AI idea. If you never write down what you learned, you relearn it. Same with people.

The notes you keep are the ones that compound.
