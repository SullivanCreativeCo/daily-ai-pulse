LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
Anthropic just launched its most powerful models and then told everyone to start with a different one.

Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 dropped Monday. Cheaper than the last version, faster too. One engineer pointed Fable at an empty folder and it ran more than 1,000 subagents for eight days. What came out was a working operating system prototype.

Wild.

But read the part right under the launch. For most coding and knowledge work, Anthropic says start with Opus 5.5. Go up to Fable for the really hard stuff. Go down to Sonnet or Haiku for the light stuff.

That's the lesson. The biggest model isn't the right model. The right model is the one that fits the job.

Most of what a small business needs from AI is not an eight-day operating system build. It's an email answered. An invoice chased. A follow-up that actually goes out.

Match the tool to the weight of the task. Save the heavy hitter for the heavy lift.

What's the hardest job you've handed an AI so far, and did it actually need the biggest model?
LI . NO. 02
Your AI assistant can probably read your inbox.

Did you decide that, or did it just kind of happen?

Dark Reading talked to Martin Zugec at Bitdefender about NIST's new small business security guide. His point stuck with me. Solopreneurs are using AI assistants to read their mailboxes, run commands, write code, and act under their own accounts. One person doing the work of a small team.

And the guide says nothing about what those agents should be allowed to reach.

That's the gap. Not a new kind of attack. Just a person moving faster than their security does.

The practical list is boring in the best way. Phishing-resistant MFA. A password manager. A standard account for daily work. Automatic updates. Backups with one copy offline. An afternoon of work.

I'd add one more for the agent era. Write down what your AI can touch. Then ask if you'd be fine with a stranger having the same access.

What can your agent reach right now that you'd be nervous to say out loud?
LI . NO. 03
A hunter couldn't get his elk calls right. So he built an app to grade them.

Ryan Matthews has hunted elk for five years. His calling never got where he wanted it. He'd send videos to a friend who's a world champion caller, get feedback, then feel bad sending another one.

Then he remembered a bird ID app that tells you what you sound like. Somebody should make that for hunters. Not just a yes or no. Real feedback on what you're doing wrong.

He's not a developer. He says he never read a line of code. By his own numbers, about 3,000 customers in roughly 110 days.

I keep coming back to where the idea came from. Not a market report. A guy brushing his teeth, annoyed at his own problem.

That's the whole origin story. He had a problem, he knew other people had it too, and he built the fix he wanted for himself.

What are you already doing for yourself that someone else would find useful?
