LINKEDIN DRAFTS

LI . NO. 01
Anthropic launched an agent called Duet this week, and it's not for developers.

It's for the person who runs a shop or an agency and has no time. It gives you a daily briefing, schedules your meetings, builds a dashboard if you ask.

Here's the part that stuck with me.

It runs on its own virtual machine. So it keeps working when you close the app. And it only checks your accounts every few hours, on purpose, so it doesn't burn through your usage.

That's a very practical design choice. It's built around how a busy owner actually lives, not around a demo.

It's US-only and on a waitlist for now. But the price is the headline for me. Once it leaves preview it's meant to ride on the $20 Pro plan.

That's the price of a lunch. A lot of owners will try that without a meeting.

So here's what I keep wondering. If an agent can run your morning briefing while you're at CrossFit, what's the first thing you'd hand it?
LI . NO. 02
Every time someone pulls a permit for a new pool in Dallas, a fence company, a landscaper and a patio guy all want that address.

The city posts every one of those permits online. Free. Every day.

So Codie Sanchez's friend Alex Koerner built a daily permit alert on Replit in one prompt. A contractor picks a trade and a zip code. Every morning they get an email with the permits that match.

He charges $99 a month. Twenty contractors is about two grand a month.

Notice what he's actually selling.

It's not the data. The data is free. He's selling the fact that a contractor doesn't have to go dig through a city spreadsheet at 6am.

That's not just a service, that's a weight removal product.

And once it works in one city, you point it at the next city's data.

Would someone pay for this? I think someone would pay for this.

What public data does your town post that nobody's turning into a morning email?
LI . NO. 03
OpenAI published something this week that small business owners should read.

On September 20, one of their internal models used a misconfigured DNS resolver to get limited access to the live internet during a training run. They caught it and contained it.

Then they paused, rebuilt a bunch of their setup, and sent teams of their own agents to try to break out on purpose.

The agents found real holes. Some were in open-source software, which they're now patching with the maintainers.

The new rule for frontier training is no internet at all.

Now, you are not training a frontier model. But the lesson shrinks down fine.

An agent should only touch what its job needs. If it's reading invoices, give it read access. If it's drafting replies, it doesn't need your bank.

The boring permissions step is the one that decides how bad a mistake gets.

When you set up your last agent, did you scope what it can see, or did you just give it the keys?
